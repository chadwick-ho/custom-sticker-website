Will a Supplement Label Fade on the Shelf? Define the Light Exposure Before Ordering

A supplement brand approves a vivid label under a desk lamp. Months later, some bottles sit in a bright shop window while the rest remain inside closed cartons. The packaging team wants to know whether the labels will look the same at sale. A digital proof can show the intended design, but it cannot establish the lightfastness of the finished print.

The purchasing decision is to define the exposure and an acceptable change, then ask the label supplier what evidence or representative sample is available for that exact construction. Indoor lighting, window-filtered daylight and direct outdoor exposure are different conditions. A generic promise that a label is “UV resistant” does not specify the color, ink, material, finish, exposure or measurement behind the claim.

Describe where the label will spend its life

Map the journey: closed storage, transport, warehouse staging, retail display and customer use. Record which label face is exposed, whether bottles rotate, whether a window or display case filters the light and the expected duration of the most demanding stage. Separate the actual expected use from a worst-case test you may request. If the retail conditions are unknown, mark them unknown and ask the distributor rather than choosing an arbitrary duration.

Not every product needs an outdoor-grade label. A bottle kept in a carton until sale presents a different question from one displayed continuously in sunlight. Indoor lighting also matters when appearance or readability is critical; do not assume that every indoor condition is equivalent to dark storage.

Xeikon describes lightfastness as resistance of a printed image to fading or discoloration under light. Its discussion concerns its own printing systems and materials. It is useful for framing the question, not a warranty for an RP label.

Ask about the whole printed construction

Record the intended face stock, inks or print method, colors, white underprint if used, and varnish or laminate. Change one of those components and the prior evidence may no longer describe the actual label. A protective film can be relevant, but a clear finish by itself does not guarantee a particular period of color stability.

Avery Dennison's outdoor-label guidance discusses the role of materials, inks and protective finishes, and notes that applications require different tests. It covers demanding outdoor equipment rather than typical supplement shelves; use it to ask better questions, not to claim a shelf life or certification for this project.

Choose the features that matter to the brand and end user: a critical brand color, small black instructions, a variable lot code or a machine-readable symbol. A label may remain attractive while a critical field loses readability, or show a visible color change that the brand still accepts. Set separate acceptance decisions for appearance and function.

Agree a comparison before the test

Ask the supplier or an appropriate test partner to identify the sample construction, exposure method, comparison sample, observation intervals and who decides the result. If a formal test is needed, obtain the applicable method and confirm its scope and current edition with the testing party. A supplier's result on another ink or material should not be presented as evidence for your own printed labels.

A simple internal comparison can help decide whether further investigation is needed, but it is not automatically an accelerated-aging test or a prediction of months on a shelf. Keep an unexposed control from the same construction, label both samples, document where each is kept and examine them under consistent viewing conditions. Record observations without converting a few days near a window into a guaranteed service life. If the decision is commercially or technically important, ask a qualified provider for an agreed test.

What should happen when appearance changes?

If an exposed sample shifts in color, compare it with the unexposed control and the brand's agreed acceptance criteria. Record whether the difference is in the ink, the label material, the finish or only an apparent change under a different light. Do not identify the mechanism from appearance alone. If instructions or codes are involved, the responsible reviewer should separately assess legibility and code quality under the applicable requirements.

Possible next steps include adjusting the packaging route, requesting evidence for another ink or finish, or agreeing a different label construction. The choice depends on the actual container and handling. A laminate can introduce other trade-offs, including later overprinting or appearance; the separate lamination and varnish guide addresses that decision.

Put the exposure in the quote request

Send RP Labels the label size, container, artwork and critical colors, expected storage and display environments, whether a barcode or later-printed field must remain readable, and the duration the buyer wants to evaluate. Ask which construction is proposed, what samples or existing evidence are available, and whether a separate test is required. Do not approve a performance claim until its scope and the responsible decision maker are clear.
