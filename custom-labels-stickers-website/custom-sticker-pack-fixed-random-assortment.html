How Many Complete Custom Sticker Packs Can You Make?

Suppose your promotion promises one fox, one owl and one bear sticker in every envelope. The stockroom has 100 foxes, 100 owls and 92 bears. You can fill 92 complete packs. After packing, eight foxes and eight owls remain. A total of 292 usable stickers sounds close to the 300 ordered, but the shortage affects eight customers. Changing what those eight people receive is a merchandising decision.

That illustrative order shows why custom sticker pack collation belongs in the quote. Collation is the job of putting the specified designs together in each pack. A printer, a separate packer and your own staff could all work from the same design files and still deliver different assortments unless someone defines one finished unit.

Write down what a customer can count on

Read the product listing as if you were opening the envelope. “Includes the three woodland characters shown” calls for a fixed assortment. Give each design an ID, approved artwork revision and quantity per pack. Use a bill of materials simple enough for a packer to read at a glance.

Design in the example Pieces per pack Usable pieces on hand Left after 92 packs
Fox 1 100 8
Owl 1 100 8
Bear 1 92 0

For any fixed mix, divide the usable quantity of each required design by its pieces per pack. Round each answer down to a whole pack. The smallest answer is your ceiling. If a different pack promises two foxes, an owl and a bear, the same inventory supports only 50 packs. That change to one line in the pack specification shifts the shortage to a different design.

Count units available for assembly after setting aside approved display samples, loose spares or visibly damaged pieces. Identify the target quantity separately, such as 100 complete packs plus a named quantity of each loose design. If a quote lists only the number of pieces printed, the buyer still needs a count for the deliverable the retailer will receive.

For a mystery pack, define what “random” permits

A random assortment also has rules. Consider the illustrative promise “three different animals in every pack, including one owl.” An envelope with two foxes and an owl has a duplicate. Three different designs without an owl leave out the character the buyer was promised. A packer needs to know the allowed design pool, the number drawn, which designs must appear and whether duplicates are allowed.

Distinguish a promise about each pack from a target across the shipment. An equal number of foxes and bears ordered does not ensure that each envelope contains one of each. If marketing promises particular odds or a rare insert, ask the person responsible for the promotion to define the distribution, counting method and records before the packer quotes it. Avoid implying a distribution the order cannot demonstrate. When a substitute would change a published promise, identify who can authorize a change and how the listing and header card will be revised.

Make the packing instruction visible at the bench

Provide a photo or drawing of one illustrative complete pack, a contents list and the correct outer package or header-card revision. If two packs share artwork, give each finished assortment a distinct pack ID and label the loose inventory by design. A sample opened at receipt can be checked against the contents list even when a character is hidden behind the others in the sealed bag. The same identity problem appears in kits with a carton and multiple component designs. Matching outer and inner pack identities

Specify what happens to short packs before dispatch. In the woodland example, the packer could hold the eight incomplete units for replacement bears and report 92 complete. Alternatively, the buyer could approve shipping those 92 with 16 loose pieces separately identified. Neither instruction turns the loose pieces into eight more finished packs. Record which party reconciles packed units and who signs off any short shipment.

Put printing and pack assembly on separate quote lines

Ask for a printing price against each design and quantity, then identify any cutting, bagging, header card, assembly and packing checks you need priced. A published example from StickerApp asks buyers to define the stickers and review the header card as part of its own pack-order workflow. Confirm directly which of those operations a prospective supplier can provide. StickerApp's pack ordering guide
