Peptide Vial Label Corners: When a Rounded Die Cut Helps a Tight Wrap

On a small glass vial, a millimeter of lifted label edge is more useful feedback than a perfect flat PDF.

Published October 3, 2026 - By RP Labels

A buyer asks for the maximum printable width on a 2 ml vial. The flat dieline is clean and the square corners look tidy. Then the first applied sample develops a tiny tab at one end. In a box of vials, that tab catches a finger long before anyone notices the typography.

I would not declare that every small vial needs rounded corners. I would put a square-cut and a rounded-cut version, in the proposed film and adhesive, on the actual glass. The corner shape is one variable in a whole label construction. A prettier cut does not compensate for a face material that resists the radius or an adhesive that cannot hold on that surface.

Start with the straight wall, not the nominal vial volume

Two vials both sold as 2 ml may not offer the same straight wall. A tall shoulder, thick base or different diameter changes the usable label area. Measure the actual glass between shoulder and base and mark where the wrap seam will land. If the label is pushed onto a curved shoulder, changing the corner radius at the opposite end will not solve the problem.

HERMA's guidance on labeling curved surfaces emphasizes flexible materials and appropriate initial and final adhesive tack for small radii. Avery Dennison's facestock guide likewise treats small diameter and container shape as selection inputs. Neither source gives a universal corner-radius number for your vial. That number belongs in a sample trial.

Test the cut in the planned application process

Prepare two otherwise identical samples. Keep the artwork, label width, material, adhesive, glass and application pressure the same; change only the corner profile. Apply them by the method you will actually use. Hand placement can put a little extra pressure on an edge that an automatic applicator will not, while a machine may reveal an unwind or positioning issue that hand application hides.

Look at the ends immediately after application, after the label has had its expected dwell time, and after the handling sequence that matters to the buyer. Photograph the same edge each time. If both profiles lift, revisit the film stiffness, surface preparation, adhesive and total wrap length before commissioning another die. If only a sharp tip catches, a radius may be a useful mechanical change.

Protect copy from the rounding and the overlap

Rounding removes printable corner area. Keep a safe inset for tiny lot text, QR artwork and color bands. A code's quiet area should not be trimmed by the die. At the other end, do not place important characters on a seam that can shift slightly as labels are applied. The buyer should review the proof as a wrapped object from front, side and seam views, not as a rectangle on screen.

There is also a tooling decision. A custom rounded die may be sensible for a recurring vial range, but a short trial order may be better served by a supplier's existing size and radius if that size leaves enough safe artwork space. Ask which dimensions are standard, which need new tooling, and whether the same cut can be reused across color variants. Do not trade away the required copy area just to avoid a one-time die decision.

When requesting a quote, send a photo or sample of the real vial, the intended finished width and height, two corner options if uncertain, the material preference, the application method and the handling conditions. Ask for an applied proof on the actual container. The result should be a small repeatable decision about edge behavior, not a promise that rounded corners alone prevent lifting.
