Printing Batch and Expiry Dates on Supplement Labels

The artwork can be approved, the bottles can be waiting and the label roll can still fail at the last meter of production: the batch code lands on a dark gradient, the ribbon will not bond to the laminate, or the roll reaches the coder in the wrong direction. The blank panel is small. Its consequences are not.

The short answer: design the batch and expiry area around the equipment that will print it later. Confirm the print technology, ribbon or ink, panel surface, text format, roll direction and verification method before the main labels are produced.

Variable data is often treated as empty space. Marketing finishes the front panel, compliance finishes the side panel, and operations receives whatever rectangle is left. By then the date coder is expected to behave like a polite guest and fit into the corner.

It usually does, until it does not. The factory can print a beautiful label that is completely unsuitable for the buyer's downstream printer. That is not a print-quality problem. It is a workflow problem that happened to arrive on a roll.

Preprint the data or add it later?

Preprinted fixed dataSimple when every label in the run carries the same approved information. It creates obsolete inventory if the batch, date or market version changes.
Downstream variable codingLets one base label serve multiple batches, but requires a compatible panel, equipment setup and controlled data entry.
Factory variable printingCan deliver numbered or batch-specific versions when the data file and responsibility are defined before production.
Separate secondary labelFlexible for small runs, but adds another application step and another chance for mismatch or crooked placement.

A young brand with one product and one pilot batch may find fixed data efficient. A contract packer running many lots across several SKUs usually values a common base label with controlled downstream coding. Neither route is automatically cheaper once obsolete stock, setup time and line changes are counted.

The twelve-SKU problem

Imagine four formulas, each in three flavors. The artwork team sees twelve labels. Operations may see dozens of batch and date combinations over a year. If every combination is preprinted, the purchasing team gets low unit prices and a shelf full of leftovers. If all twelve SKUs share a disciplined coding panel and roll format, more of the inventory remains usable.

The opposite mistake also happens. A buyer combines everything into one base design, then discovers that staff must choose from too many fields at the line. The money saved in print versions returns as slower setup and greater selection risk. Consolidation is useful only when the operating process can control it.

This is the part of label sales that rarely makes a product photo. It is also where a practical supplier can save more than the difference between matte and gloss.

Build the coding panel around the printer

Thermal transfer, inkjet and other coding systems have different requirements. The printable surface must accept the selected ribbon or ink. The code needs enough contrast. The print head needs a stable area, and the sensor needs a reliable gap or mark. High speed reduces tolerance for vague positioning.

Do not approve a glossy laminate and then ask whether the date will stick to it. Ask before the laminate is chosen. Sometimes a dedicated unlaminated or specially coated panel is appropriate. Sometimes a compatible ribbon solves the issue. The answer belongs to the exact construction and equipment, not a general internet rule.

Buying rule: the coding box is not leftover artwork space. It is a small machine interface and should be specified with the same care as the roll core and unwind direction.

Roll specifications that matter

  • Unwind direction: determines which edge enters the coder or applicator first.
  • Core size: must fit the equipment and provide stable roll handling.
  • Maximum roll diameter: affects whether the finished roll physically fits the machine.
  • Web width and position: place the coding panel under the available print area.
  • Label gap or pitch: supports sensing, registration and repeat accuracy.
  • Outside or inside winding: changes how the label presents to the print head.

A PDF proof can show orientation, but a machine sample confirms feeding. If the buyer has an equipment diagram, an old working roll or a short video of the web path, send it before production. Those files are less glamorous than a brand deck and more useful to the press team.

What quality control should verify

  1. Correct batch, lot and date format against the controlled job record.
  2. Readable contrast and character shape at normal production speed.
  3. Position within the reserved panel across the beginning, middle and end of the run.
  4. Rub and handling resistance after the ink or ribbon has set.
  5. Barcode or 2D-code verification where the variable field includes machine-readable data.
  6. Reconciliation of used, rejected and remaining labels when traceability requires it.

The printer supplies the approved label and can support printability. The buyer remains responsible for the accuracy and regulatory suitability of product data. Keep that boundary clear. A helpful layout review should not quietly become approval of claims, dates or release status.

What to send before the proof

Variable-data label brief

  • Printer or coder model and print technology
  • Ribbon, ink or consumable currently used
  • Exact batch, lot and expiry format with maximum character count
  • Required panel size, location and contrast
  • Core, roll diameter, web width, gap and unwind direction
  • Line speed and hand, coder or applicator sequence
  • Number of SKUs and which artwork elements change
  • Quantity per version and reorder plan

FAQ

Should batch and expiry dates be preprinted with the label?

Only when the information is fixed and the inventory risk is acceptable. Variable production data is often added later so one base label can support multiple batches.

Can I print dates over a laminated label?

Possibly, but ink or ribbon compatibility must be tested. A reserved printable panel or compatible coating is safer than assuming every laminate accepts downstream coding.

Why does roll direction matter for date coding?

The coding area must arrive under the print head in the correct orientation and position. Core size, roll width, gap, winding and sensor marks may also affect the equipment setup.

What should be included in a coding sample test?

Use the production printer, consumable, speed and final label construction. Check registration, contrast, rub resistance, readability and verification after the planned handling cycle.
