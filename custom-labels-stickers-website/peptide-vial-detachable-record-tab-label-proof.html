Peptide Vial Labels with Detachable Record Tabs: Proof Both Identities

A tear-off tab only saves time if the identity left on the vial and the identity moved to the record stay unambiguous.

Published October 6, 2026 - By RP Labels

A research vial is labeled once, then somebody needs the same identifier on a sample log or carton record. A detachable tab sounds like an easy answer. The expensive mistake is approving only the picture of a tab. The part that remains on glass, the part that peels away, and the record that receives it all have to tell the same story.

If I were buying this label, I would start with a physical mockup rather than a roll quote. Ask who removes the tab, when they do it, what surface they attach it to, and whether the vial will still be identifiable afterward. A short ID may be enough for an internal record, but only if your own process says so. Do not silently replace a full vial identity with a tiny detachable number.

Choose what is repeated before drawing the perforation

Write the identity fields in two columns: stay-on-vial and transfer-to-record. Product name, batch or sample identifier, and any variable code should be assigned deliberately. If a record tab carries a shortened code, document how that code maps back to the vial. If the code is unique per vial, the supplier needs a data file and an agreed sequence; if it is only a batch reference, do not call it unit serialization.

HERMA's documentation-label overview explains the basic construction: parts of an applied main label can be detached and moved to another surface. Its vial-labeling application page also notes detachable documentation labels on vials. Those examples establish that the format is real, not that any off-the-shelf die cut will suit a particular research vial or record card.

Proof the peel, not just the printing

The tab must peel without pulling the whole wrap off the small glass body. It must also stick to the destination card or document. Ask for both actions to be tested after the label has been applied for a realistic amount of time. A tab that peels beautifully immediately after application may behave differently after storage; the acceptance condition needs to be specified by the buyer.

Check where the die cut runs relative to the wrap seam and the curved bottle. A narrow tear line can cut into a code, leave a sharp corner or expose an edge that catches during tray packing. Inspect the liner under the tab as well: a die that cuts too deeply may make roll dispensing unreliable. This is a converting question, not something a PDF can prove.

Keep the two copies easy to reconcile

Use a deliberately simple test set: two different product names, the longest permitted batch string, and several consecutive identifiers if variable data is planned. Apply the labels to actual vials, detach the tabs in a mixed group, and try to match each tab to the correct vial and record without relying on memory. That exercise often exposes tiny type or insufficient distinguishing information before a full roll is printed.

The RP Research image here is an AI-generated demonstration. Its repeated DEMO lot should be read as a layout example, not as evidence of unique serialization. A production workflow needs a documented data source, a print check and a reconciliation plan if uniqueness matters. Keep the approved sample and the data template together so a reorder does not quietly change one while leaving the other unchanged.

Send one complete purchasing brief

Provide the actual vial and cap, dimensions, intended application method, tab destination surface, stay-on and removable text, variable-data format, roll direction, expected quantities and sample acceptance steps. Ask the converter to quote the finished construction and a physical trial. A quote for a normal wrap label with a dotted line drawn in the artwork is not the same product.

I would approve the job only when someone who was not in the design meeting can remove a tab, place it on the record, and still identify the vial confidently. The point of the extra die-cut work is less transcription and fewer mix-ups, not another decorative feature on a tiny bottle.
