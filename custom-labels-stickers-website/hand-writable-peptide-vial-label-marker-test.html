Hand-Writable Peptide Vial Labels: Test the Marker Before You Order

The lot field can be perfectly placed on the proof and still smear when someone writes on the finished vial.

The question is not whether the proof has room for a lot number. It is whether the person at the bench can write it on the finished label, read it later, and keep it there through normal handling. A tiny vial makes that surprisingly easy to overlook.

I would start with the actual marker and the actual vial, not a generic promise that a film is "writable." The face stock, varnish or laminate, pen chemistry, hand pressure and time before the vial is touched all affect the result. The printable artwork and the write-on field are two different surfaces to approve.

Reserve a field someone can actually use

Put the handwritten field where a gloved operator can reach it after the label is applied. Leave room for the longest expected lot or date string, including a correction or a second line if your procedure permits one. Do not make a narrow gap between a QR graphic and a seam the only available writing space.

Before choosing a finish, make a full-size print and ask the operator to write the longest realistic entry. The bottle should be held the way it will be held in the packing workflow. If the marker rides over the curve or into the overlap, the problem is geometry, not ink. The size chart and gap-versus-overlap guide help set that geometry before proof approval.

Why a glossy protective finish may change the answer

A protective laminate can make the printed brand panel more robust, but it does not automatically make the label a good writing surface. Avery's guidance for its overlaminated products says permanent marker is easiest across that range, while ballpoint and pencil are recommended only on its matte versions. That is product-specific guidance, not a guarantee for another converter's film.

Avery also warns that gel and rollerball pens can have poor writeability and long dry times on its printed labels. I would treat that as a reason to test, not as a reason to ban every pen. Ask for two candidate constructions and compare them with the exact marker the team already uses. A printed matte patch within an otherwise protected design may be a better compromise than removing protection from the entire label.

Run a small, repeatable marker trial

Write a short field and the longest expected field on each construction. Record the marker brand and tip, the approximate wait before touching, and whether the vial was clean and dry. Then handle the vial as usual, place it in its carton or tray, and inspect for transfer, feathering, smudge and lost readability. If cold storage or condensation is part of your real route, repeat the trial after that exposure. Do not invent a cold test when the product never sees one.

Compare the handwritten mark with the preprinted product identity. A bold lot field is useful only if it does not obscure the vial's name, warning or other approved copy. It also needs a clear instruction for who writes it and when. A blank write-on field is not a substitute for an approved variable-data printing workflow when every vial needs a controlled identifier; see the variable-data release checklist for that case.

My approval point is a labeled vial and a short roll sample, not a screenshot of a blank field. Send RP the vial size, intended copy, marker and handling route. Ask for a candidate construction and a proof that leaves the write-on area usable in practice.
