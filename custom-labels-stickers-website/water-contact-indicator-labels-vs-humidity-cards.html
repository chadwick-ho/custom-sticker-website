Water Contact Indicator Labels vs. Humidity Cards

If the receiving team needs to know whether liquid water reached a particular spot, put a water contact indicator at that spot. If it needs to know whether the air inside a sealed bag reached a specified relative humidity (RH) range, choose a humidity indicator card for that range. The two can be used together, but give each its own location, reading instructions, and reason to hold a shipment.

Decide what the receiver needs to know

Receiving question What to specify What the reading cannot establish
Did liquid water reach this point? A water contact indicator with a documented response, visible at the chosen wetting point. Where the water came from, when it arrived, or whether the rest of the shipment was wet.
Did the air at the card reach a specified RH range? A humidity indicator card with the required spots and a supplier-defined reading method, positioned in the headspace you want to assess. Whether a particular surface got wet, or how RH changed over time.

For example, 3M's 5557 technical data sheet describes a specific tape with an irreversible water-contact color change and a design intended to tolerate humidity aging without giving a water indication. That is not a specification for every contact label or converted version of that tape. Desiccare's humidity indicator cards illustrate a different output: color spots interpreted against stated RH bands. Confirm whether the selected card can revert and how soon it must be read once exposed to room air. If the buyer needs a time-stamped history or duration, specify a suitable logger instead of asking a passive spot to provide one. The indicator-versus-logger guide explains that distinction using temperature monitoring.

Mark the wetting point and headspace separately

For a contact label, mark the point whose wetting would trigger a receiving action. A spot near a seam and a spot beside an inner tray answer different questions. On a mock pack, check whether the chosen spot can actually be wetted and whether receivers can see the sensing area before disturbing the evidence. For an RH card, choose a position open to the air volume being monitored; don't tuck it under a liner or against a desiccant sachet if the goal is to assess the general headspace. Follow the card supplier's placement and viewing instructions.

Run three controls on separate mock packs

  • Dry control. Use the proposed pack materials, indicator lots, and locations without the intended challenge. Record the starting appearance and verify that the reading and handling procedure itself does not introduce a flag.
  • Elevated RH, no condensation. Set the RH condition with the buyer's quality team and document it with an independent reference and a check for condensation. Compare the card with its supplier's expected indication under the agreed conditions. Check the contact label separately against its claimed behavior under non-condensing humidity.
  • Local liquid contact. Wet only the defined challenge point in an inert mock pack and document where the water reached. Compare the contact label at that location with its supplier's readout criteria. Do not interpret a card that was directly wetted as a valid RH reading unless its instructions explicitly support that interpretation.

Record the indicator part and lot, the pack revision, starting and final appearance, location, time relative to opening, and whether each control behaved as expected. Keep the condensation scenario separate if it matters to the shipment: condensed water is liquid at the surface, so a contact indication alone cannot distinguish condensation from a leak or splash.

Write the hold rule before requesting samples

Define the readout that triggers a hold, who reviews it, and what to do if the indicator is missing or unreadable. For a flagged shipment, record the pack ID, position, read time, and a photo; route it to the quality owner alongside independent evidence such as closure condition and shipment records. An unflagged spot does not clear the rest of the pack or establish that a closure is leak-tight. See the separate guide to tamper evidence versus closure integrity.
