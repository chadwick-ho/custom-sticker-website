What Are the Odds of Accepting a Label Lot Under AQL Inspection?

Suppose a buyer samples 50 labels, finds zero nonconforming labels and accepts the shipment. Another buyer samples 100 and also requires zero to accept. How differently do the two rules behave if each label has a 1% probability of being nonconforming? Under a simple independent-sampling model, the first rule accepts about 60.50% of the time. The second accepts about 36.60%. The underlying quality is the same in both calculations. What changed was the buyer's decision rule.

That mathematical example illustrates the question to ask when a quote says “AQL inspection.” Which lot, quality level, sample size and acceptance rule does the supplier mean, and what risks do they leave with each party?

Read one point on the operating characteristic curve

An operating characteristic (OC) curve describes a sampling plan. Pick a hypothetical fraction of nonconforming units on the horizontal axis. The height of the curve gives the probability that this particular rule will accept a lot at that quality level. Each curve represents a different rule. Reading a point requires knowing both the sample size, n, and the largest number of nonconforming sample units that still allows acceptance, c. NIST explanation of OC curves

For the two illustrative rules above, c equals zero. Each sampled label must conform for the lot to be accepted. If the chance that any one label is nonconforming is p, its probability of conforming is 1 − p. With independent selections under a common probability, multiply that probability by itself n times. That gives P(accept) = (1 − p)ⁿ.

Hypothetical probability per label Inspect 50, accept on zero Inspect 100, accept on zero
0.1% 95.12% chance of acceptance 90.48% chance of acceptance
1% 60.50% 36.60%
2% 36.42% 13.26%

At the middle row, the calculation for 50 is 0.99 raised to the 50th power. A particular shipment will be accepted or rejected once. The percentages describe how often the rules would accept under repeated sampling at the stated quality. An acceptance result alone cannot determine the percentage of faulty labels left on a roll. These numbers illustrate the model and describe no supplier's output.

Put two quality points in the discussion

The AQL, or acceptable quality level in the NIST terminology, is a quality point used to evaluate a plan. At an agreed good-quality point, the producer wants a high probability of acceptance. The remaining chance of rejection there is the producer's risk. At an agreed poor-quality point, the buyer wants a low probability of acceptance. The chance of acceptance at that point is the consumer's risk. Asking for both points helps the parties discuss what a proposed inspection can actually discriminate. NIST definitions of sampling risks and quality levels

For example, if the parties chose the first and last rows of the table as those two illustrative points, the 50-label rule would reject about 4.88% at the good point and accept about 36.42% at the poor point. The 100-label rule lowers the latter chance to about 13.26%, while raising the former to about 9.52%. Whether that tradeoff is acceptable depends on the cost and consequences of each decision. An AQL entry on an order grants no allowance to ship a known fraction of defective labels.

Check whether the sample represents the rolls you receive

The calculation assumes random sampling and a large lot in which the binomial model with a common, independent defect probability is appropriate. If a print fault affects a continuous run on one roll, adjacent labels can share the same defect. Pulling a strip from the outside of one carton may then tell you much more about that strip than about the rest of the lot. For a small finite lot sampled without replacement, the quality owner may need a different calculation.

Define the unit being counted. One label with both a scuff and missing text can be one nonconforming label in a plan that counts units. A scheme that counts individual defects answers a different question. Agree how the inspector classifies each problem, identifies the lot, chooses accessible sample positions and records exclusions. A cosmetic shade variation and a missing product identity can warrant different controls because the consequences differ. Defining a label inspection scope

Before requesting a quote, ask your quality owner for the applicable plan or standard, lot size, rules by defect class and disposition after rejection. Specify any feature requiring a separate control or full inspection, who authorizes release, and which records should accompany the shipment. Send those requirements with your label specification, quantities and roll configuration so the supplier can confirm the scope it can quote. Discuss inspection requirements for a label order
