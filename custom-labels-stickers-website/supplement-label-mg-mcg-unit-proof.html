mg vs mcg on Supplement Labels: Proof the Number and Unit Together

Proof the amount, unit and serving basis together. If the approved file says 400 micrograms and the artwork says 400 milligrams, matching the digits has not preserved the amount. Hold that line and return the discrepancy to the responsible copy or regulatory owner. The designer should reproduce the approved declaration, not decide a replacement unit or recompute the nutrition panel.

For a supplement packaging buyer, this is a print-release question: which exact number-and-unit string belongs to this SKU and proof? It is separate from deciding the formulation or recommended intake.

Compare the full declared field

Use the approved copy revision for the exact product and market. For each relevant line, record the ingredient name, amount, unit, serving basis and associated notes. Compare those fields with the returned artwork at finished size, including the decimal point and any leading zero.

NIST's SI prefix table defines milli as one thousandth and micro as one millionth. Consequently, 1 milligram equals 1,000 micrograms as a mass relationship. The diagram uses 0.4 mg = 400 micrograms solely to demonstrate that changing unit representation requires the corresponding number to be considered.

The mathematical relationship does not select a permitted declaration, rounding method or nutrient-equivalence basis. For U.S. supplement panels, the applicable requirements in 21 CFR 101.36 belong with the qualified reviewer. That rule uses mcg for micrograms in relevant text; the SI symbol μg also expresses the microgram concept. Match the job's approved notation instead of silently replacing one form with another.

A unit-only edit can be a content change

In a second fictional comparison, 400 micrograms and 400 milligrams keep the same digits but represent different masses: the latter is 1,000 times the former. This is not a supplement dose example. It shows why a font change, abbreviation replacement or manual retyping must not be treated as harmless when the unit changes.

Number, unit and serving basis all match Continue the remaining copy and layout checks Designated release owner
Unit changed while digits stayed the same Hold the affected line; identify the exact source and proof Copy/data owner and qualified reviewer
A different mass unit is intentionally proposed Request reviewer-approved final display text, including rounding and basis Qualified reviewer
The unit is IU, RAE, DFE or another nutrient-specific basis Do not apply a plain mg/mcg mass conversion as if it settled the declaration Responsible nutrient-data and regulatory owner

The table does not provide activity-unit or nutrient-equivalence conversions. Those depend on the applicable ingredient and declaration rules. Likewise, changing a per-serving statement to a per-unit statement is a different calculation and approval; the per-unit/per-serving guide covers that separate denominator question.

Read the actual export and the physical proof

First compare the controlled copy with the production export the converter receives. Then inspect the returned proof at the actual label size. Confirm the unit is not missing, mistaken for another letter, separated from its number by a line break or hidden at the wrap seam. The font handoff guide addresses the rendering route; multilingual data integrity addresses characters damaged during transfer.

If the approved source is itself unclear, leave that entry unresolved and request a corrected authorized line. Do not choose whichever unit makes the value look familiar. Recheck the affected row and its neighbors after a new export, and retain both the accepted copy revision and the proof revision in the order record.

For custom supplement labels, prepare the inquiry with the bottle/dieline, quantities by SKU, approved panel file, marked mismatches and the person who owns final declared values. Ask for a print proof against that exact revision. Label printing does not calculate or approve a formula, nutrient amount or intake recommendation.
