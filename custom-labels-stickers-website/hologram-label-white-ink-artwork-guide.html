Hologram Label White Ink Artwork Guide

Holographic film should not shine through every pixel simply because it can. White ink is the control layer: it decides where brand colors stay stable, where copy remains readable and where the rainbow effect earns attention.

The short answer: place opaque white under logos, light colors, small copy and QR or barcode fields that must read consistently. Omit white under selected shapes where the holographic film should remain visible. Keep the white separation on its own named spot-color layer and approve a physical or moving-light sample.

A buyer chooses holographic film because it moves. The designer then covers the entire label with process color and is surprised when every color moves too. Another designer adds solid white beneath everything and turns the expensive holographic stock into a narrow rainbow border.

Both files are printable. Neither has made a decision.

Why process color changes over holographic film

Process inks are not completely opaque. Printed directly on reflective film, they mix visually with the rainbow substrate. Yellow can appear gold, pink can appear metallic and black may take on a gunmetal movement.

This can be beautiful in bold areas. It can also damage brand color and small text. The artwork needs a map of controlled and uncontrolled reflection.

What white ink does

White ink blocks the holographic background and creates a neutral base. Color printed over that base behaves more like color on white material. White can also print as a visible design element.

Opacity matters. A weak white layer may still allow the substrate to influence the result. Press process, ink laydown and print sequence should remain part of the approved construction.

Buying rule: every important element should have one declared behavior: opaque, metallic-colored, bare holographic or transparent. If the proof cannot answer which one it is, the artwork is not finished.

Where I normally keep white ink

  • Small product and legal copy that must remain readable.
  • Brand colors that should not change with viewing angle.
  • White logos, icons and negative spaces.
  • QR codes, barcodes and their quiet zones.
  • Serial-number fields and variable-data panels.

Large decorative lines, shield edges, stars or selected logo segments can remain unbacked so the optical material becomes part of the identity.

The startup seal and the security seal

A beauty startup wants a vivid falcon logo on a carton seal. It can use selective white under the brand name and let the wings show rainbow movement. The job is decorative, and the design can take some visual risk.

A warranty label has a different burden. Staff must read a serial number and scan a code under warehouse lighting. That information needs controlled contrast, while holographic movement can sit around it as an inspection feature. The functional panel should not compete with the effect.

Create a separate spot-white layer

Build the white mask as vector artwork on a clearly named spot-color layer according to the printer's specification. Use a visible preview color on screen, but understand that the swatch represents white ink on press.

Keep the dieline and white layer separate. Use overprint settings only as directed. Send editable artwork or a production PDF that preserves spot separations rather than a flattened JPG.

Trap for registration

White and CMYK are printed in separate passes or stations. If their edges match with zero allowance, a small registration shift can create a white halo or holographic fringe. The prepress team may choke the white mask slightly inside colored shapes.

Tiny text and hairline graphics leave little room for trapping. Simplify where necessary and approve at final size, not only in a magnified PDF.

Codes need a quiet, non-reflective field

Holographic movement and glare can interfere with camera-based scanning. Put a stable opaque backing beneath the entire code and quiet zone. Keep it away from tight curves, cut edges and tamper fractures.

Test representative final-size codes on the actual label and package with multiple phones or the intended scanner. A successful scan from the design monitor proves nothing.

Approve motion, opacity and angle

A static digital proof can show the white mask but cannot reproduce diffractive movement. Review a video under changing light and, for an important project, a physical sample. Tilt it, place it on the final black, white or colored package and inspect it at ordinary viewing distance.

Ask the supplier to identify which areas are white-backed. If the answer depends on guessing from the image, stop before bulk production.

Holographic white-ink checklist

  • Holographic pattern or custom-master direction
  • Elements intended to remain optically exposed
  • Opaque logos, text and brand-color zones
  • White-backed QR, barcode and serial fields
  • Separate named spot-white layer
  • Trapping and minimum-detail review
  • Dieline, bleed and tamper construction
  • Moving-light and final-package approval

FAQ

What does white ink do on a holographic label?

Opaque white blocks the reflective substrate and creates a stable base for normal-looking color, white text and scanning areas.

Where should holographic film remain exposed?

Leave white out of intentional windows, bold logo elements or patterns where optical movement supports the design without reducing readability.

Does CMYK print the color white?

No. Standard CMYK white is normally the absence of ink. White on holographic material requires a separate white separation.

Can a QR code print directly over holographic film?

Reflective movement can reduce contrast and create glare. A controlled opaque backing and final-size scan test are safer.
