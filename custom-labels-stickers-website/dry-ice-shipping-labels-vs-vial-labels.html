Dry Ice Shipping Labels or Vial Labels? Specify the Package Each One Identifies

A purchase order says “dry ice labels.” The packaging buyer means identification labels for cold-handled research vials. The shipping team means transport marks and labels on the outer shipper. Both teams can receive attractive print while the original requirement remains unresolved.

Name the packaging layer and purpose before ordering. A vial identity label and dry ice transport marking have different briefs. The shipping party responsible for the actual route, service and contents must determine the applicable transport requirements and supply the approved shipping-artwork instructions. A vial-print approval does not authorize a shipment.

Point to the vial, product carton and outer shipper

Mark each print request on a package photograph or a simple layer map. The vial carries product identity. A product carton may organize the associated product information. The outer transport package has its own shipping identification, marks and labels as applicable. These surfaces should have separate names in the order.

A branded product carton is not automatically the outer shipper. Likewise, a label reading a storage instruction on a vial supplies static information; it does not fulfill the outer package's transport-marking assignment. Describe each component by its actual role, even when several components are rectangular boxes.

Use the vial-and-product-carton design guide to coordinate product identity across those two components. The shipping brief needs a separate description of the transport package. If the only purchase is vial identification, keep that order specific instead of buying an unspecified “dry ice label set.”

Establish the complete route with the shipping owner

Give the responsible shipping team the actual transport modes, origin and destination, carrier/service, contents and planned refrigerant quantity. Include connecting stages. A road pickup before an airport does not make the full movement a ground-only service, and “research material” is not a completed classification of the contents.

PHMSA interpretation 23-0012, issued September 29, 2023, discusses dry ice's air/vessel entries and motor-vehicle shipping-paper questions, including multimodal transport. It illustrates why mode and the full transport scenario matter. It is not a universal artwork specification or a ruling on the buyer's shipment.

Service rules also need their own scope. For example, USPS Packaging Instruction 9A prohibits dry ice in international mail and distinguishes restricted domestic air and surface mailing conditions. Those are USPS provisions. They should not become a statement that every carrier prohibits international dry ice movements or uses the same limits and documents.

Ask the shipping owner to resolve the applicable classification, service acceptance, marks, labels and documentation under the current requirements. Purchasing should receive the resulting print instructions, rather than asking the designer to choose a generic online graphic.

Distinguish fixed artwork from entries that change by shipment

Have the shipping team identify which information belongs in approved fixed artwork and which must be supplied for each shipment. Confirm the process for variable entries, including who supplies and verifies them. A value that changes with the shipment should not be preprinted as a permanent fact merely because the first order used it.

Keep the approved artwork revision with its intended package and route/service scope. If a supplier offers a stock transport label, ask the shipping owner whether its exact content and format suit the intended use. A recognizable hazard graphic on a catalog page does not settle that question.

The print file and any shipment-specific completion process must work together. Do not put example quantities, copied shipping descriptions or placeholder values into a production release. If the instructions are incomplete, the affected outer-package print scope remains unresolved while the team closes the missing inputs.

Turn one ambiguous purchase line into two defined scopes

The following two purchase-order lines are editorial examples for organizing procurement. They contain no approved transport artwork, classification or completed shipping information.

PO line A — Research vial identification labels. Print the supplied product-identity artwork at its approved revision for the identified actual vial. State the label dimensions, quantity for each design, application method and relevant handling/storage sequence. Request the proposed construction and applied-sample checks for those conditions.

PO line B — Outer-shipper print. Request print against the shipping owner's approved artwork and variable-entry plan for the identified transport-package surface. State the required finished size, quantity and applicable route/service reference supplied by that owner. Keep this line unreleased until the artwork, entry responsibilities and supplier's offered scope are confirmed.

The two lines may go to different suppliers. If only one scope is required, buy that scope. If both are required, list them separately even when one supplier proposes both. Sharing a purchase order should not conceal which party approved the transport content.

Check print reproduction and the intended surface

For the outer-package request, supply the actual surface, finished artwork size and placement instructions from the shipping owner. Ask the proposed print supplier to flag reproduction or application constraints. Return any proposed resizing, rearrangement or substitution for the appropriate review; shrinking transport artwork to the dimensions of a vial sticker changes the brief.

Have the shipping team review placement alongside other required package information. The print supplier should receive resolved instructions for what must stay visible, rather than improvising space around an address label. Identify relevant use conditions and agree suitable sample checks for the proposed surface and construction.

For the vial label, evaluate its own fit, readability and application sequence. The refrigerated versus frozen label guide helps describe those material-use questions. Label durability evidence does not establish transport eligibility or the correctness of outer-package marks.

Send the vial brief and retain the shipping review

For RP custom vial labels, send the actual vial, approved identity artwork, quantities per design, application method and handling conditions. Identify any matching product-carton labels separately.

Prepare a vial-label inquiry for that ordinary print work. If you also have an outer-package print request, describe it as a separate proposed scope and obtain explicit supplier confirmation against the shipping owner's resolved instructions. Keep transport classification, packaging, service acceptance and shipping documents with the responsible shipping process. Finished printed marks are one component of that process, not evidence that the whole shipment is ready to dispatch.
