Braille Supplement Labels: Define the Message and Prove It by Touch

A brand wants people to distinguish two similar supplement bottles by touch. The designer proposes a braille font; another proposal adds a raised logo. Which one answers the brief?

If the intended task is reading a product identity in braille, specify the message, obtain specialist transcription review, and evaluate a tactile specimen on the actual bottle with appropriate readers. Visible dots and decorative relief do not demonstrate touch reading. Start with what someone needs to identify, then decide how the package will carry that information.

Name the bottle-identification task

Write the task in ordinary language: “Distinguish these two approved product variants when selecting one bottle independently.” Identify the products, intended readers and setting. Decide which words distinguish the variants without asking a reader to infer the answer from color or a familiar logo.

Braille is one possible identification method. RNIB's labeling guidance describes braille, tactile markers, large print and audio options. A locating bump and a message that can be read are different deliverables, and readers' preferred methods vary. Establish that the chosen approach serves the intended users before allocating the label area.

For this brief, keep the task to bottle identification. A short tactile identifier should not be assumed to communicate directions, warnings or all other product information. Preserve the applicable approved visual copy and plan any additional accessible information through the product team's own review.

Approve the English transcription before fitting the artwork

Give the braille specialist the exact approved source words, including the variant that differentiates the bottles. Request a corresponding transcription reference and the code conventions and production parameters to use. Have abbreviations or shortened messages reviewed explicitly; a designer should not invent them to save space.

Changing a visual font to a dotted font leaves both content and physical output unanswered. The source words, specialist-reviewed transcription and actual production message must correspond. Keep that correspondence available to the artwork reviewer without making visual recognition of dots the acceptance test.

The Braille Authority of North America explanation of size and spacing describes braille as touch reading, with fixed-width cells and dots distinguishable from their background. Those physical relationships explain why a flat PDF or a scaled-down pattern cannot prove usability. Its paper and signage discussion is not a bottle-specific production specification; agree the appropriate requirements for the proposed package with the specialist.

Select a location a reader can find and use

Map the candidate tactile area on the actual bottle. Consider the curve beneath the fingers, the label seam, edges, other raised decoration and the normal grip. Show how the reader is expected to locate and orient the message without relying solely on a printed arrow.

Do not treat braille as visual type that can be reduced until it fits. If the reviewed message cannot occupy a usable area, reconsider the message or carrier with the specialist and readers. A different package component may be worth proposing, but it changes where the user must look and what remains available after unpacking.

Separate the tactile zone from decorative elements on the drawing. The embossed and debossed supplement-label guide addresses relief as a decorative and applied-construction question. A pleasing raised logo provides no evidence that a neighboring braille message has the correct content or is readable.

Use an actual-bottle task to review the tactile specimen

First have the specialist review the proposed transcription and physical output. Then arrange reader evaluation using identified specimens on the intended bottles. Record the sample's artwork, construction, tactile process, location and any difference from the proposed production version.

Use this observation sequence rather than a photograph approval:

  1. Ask the reader to locate the identification area under the agreed use conditions.
  2. Record how the message was oriented and what the reader actually read.
  3. Check whether that reading distinguishes the intended bottle from the other variant.
  4. Record difficulty, uncertainty or confusion and return the affected message, position or construction for revision.

Agree the review method with the specialist and intended readers. This sequence supplies procurement questions, not a universal test protocol or a claimed pass rate. The digital-proof versus physical-sample guide explains why layout evidence and physical evidence answer different questions; a screen image cannot provide tactile evidence.

Follow two proposed bottles without inventing a result

In an editorial example, DEMO-A and DEMO-B have similar visual packaging. The brand supplies distinct approved identification words. A specialist is asked to prepare the two corresponding English braille references, and packaging proposes a candidate area on each bottle. No transcription or reading trial has been completed in this example.

The review record should connect each approved print source to its braille reference and specimen. Leave the observation fields—how it was located, what was read and what needs changing—for the actual evaluation. If readers cannot find the area, correct transcription alone has left the identification task unresolved. If both bottles are read as the same variant, investigate the source, transcription and output before changing only the location.

After the proposed packing and handling sequence, recheck the same identification task on representative specimens. A cover, abrasion or compression may change the usable surface. Reorders also need the correct message for each SKU, even when bottle dimensions and decorative artwork are shared.

Quote the printed layer with the tactile scope identified

For an RP supplement-label printing inquiry, send the actual bottle, approved visual copy, quantity per design, application method and intended handling. Add the identification task, specialist-reviewed tactile references when available and proposed location. Identify which party is supplying the transcription, tactile production and reader evaluation.

Prepare the supplement label brief and request a defined proposal for the ordinary printed-label work and its interface with the tactile area. Confirm separately which specialist work is included in any supplier's offer. Production release should follow the agreed content and actual-bottle evidence for the chosen identification task.
