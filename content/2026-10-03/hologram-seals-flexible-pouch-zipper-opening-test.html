A hologram seal can look convincing across a pouch in a product photo. Then a customer pinches the zipper, the soft film flexes, and the seal peels from one side without following the opening path. The problem is not that the rainbow effect was too weak. The closure geometry and the real pouch surface were never tested together.

I would define the job first: is the label intended to show first opening, temporarily hold a fold, or allow repeated resealing? Those are different constructions. A permanent, evidence-oriented hologram seal should not be described as a convenient resealable tab, and a removable closure label should not be sold as tamper evidence without a verified construction.

Map the actual opening motion

Mark where the zipper sits, where the user's fingers grip, and which surface changes shape as the pouch opens. A seal placed across two moving faces may wrinkle before opening; a seal placed only on one face may do nothing to reveal that the zipper was opened. A tear line, if used, has to intersect the real opening path rather than merely look centered in flat artwork.

HERMA's closure-label example describes how uneven package surfaces, dust, grip tabs and adhesive selection affect closure behavior. Its flexible-bag example demonstrates that a label for repeated opening is designed differently from a one-time seal. These are material-selection lessons, not evidence that any particular RP hologram has passed a security test.

Try the film and adhesive on the filled pouch

Ask for pouches from the actual filling run, not just empty smooth samples. The fill changes the tension at the top seam, while heat sealing, powder dust or handling can change the surface. HERMA's material range distinguishes seal and security adhesive constructions; the exact choice still depends on your film and desired removal behavior.

Apply several sample seals with the intended pressure and dwell time. Open the pouches the way a customer would: pull from a corner, pinch the zipper, flex the gusset and try a slow peel. Note whether the label tears, leaves visible residue, lifts cleanly, damages the pouch or interferes with the zipper. A product photo cannot answer those questions.

Run the trial on more than one pouch from the same production lot. Pouch thickness and the position of the heat-seal edge can vary enough to change how a short label bridges the opening. If the hologram is applied by machine, test placement tolerance on a filled pouch; if it is applied by hand, record the position that workers can reproduce without covering the zipper's grip area.

Keep the approved pouch, seal sample and opening photographs with the reorder brief. They give the next print run a physical reference, not just a color PDF.

Do not let the hologram hide the evidence

The optical effect belongs where the customer can see it, but the evidence feature needs to be visible too. If a VOID or destructible construction is proposed, test whether its residue or break pattern is readable on the chosen dark or glossy film. If a perforation is proposed, test its tear direction. In either case, avoid claiming "tamper-proof" or regulatory compliance based on a graphic rendering.

Send the supplier the pouch material, filled and empty samples, zipper/heat-seal drawing, expected storage and handling conditions, target seal position, application process and the evidence you want after opening. Request a physical trial before buying a roll. The picture in this guide is a visual concept, not a documented security-performance result.
