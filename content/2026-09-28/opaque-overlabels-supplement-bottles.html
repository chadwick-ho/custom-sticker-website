An opaque sticker is not a time machine. It can hide a printed line on an old supplement bottle. It cannot, by itself, make the replacement copy correct, the adhesive compatible or the finished pack suitable for sale.

When a packaging revision arrives after bottles have been labeled, the easy question is “Can you cover this?” The useful questions are which units are affected, exactly what must change, who approved the new statement, and whether the bottle can still carry one unambiguous set of information. This is a packaging-production guide, not legal approval for a relabeling campaign.

First decide whether an overlabel is appropriate

If the error involves a critical claim, required declaration, ingredient statement or safety information, have the responsible regulatory and quality teams review the correction plan for the market where the product will be sold. Do not ask a printer to decide whether a revised dietary supplement label meets law. The FDA dietary supplement labeling guide is a useful US starting point, but it is not a substitute for product- and market-specific review.

A small promotional date change is not the same risk as contradictory ingredient information. Some inventory should be reworked; some should be held or replaced. I would settle that decision before asking a converter for a thousand cover-up labels. A fast quote is not a release decision.

Opacity is a material specification, not a promise from a mockup

White print over old dark text can look opaque on a screen and still show the underlying copy in daylight. Avery Dennison's cover-up range describes high-opacity materials designed to conceal outdated information and emphasizes that overlabeling must really hide the old print. Ask for the proposed face material and opacity construction, not merely “white BOPP.”

Test the new label directly on the darkest old artwork, on the actual amber or clear bottle, under strong light and after the expected handling interval. Look for shadows of old letters through light areas, especially near the edges. Also check whether the old barcode or QR code remains partly visible. Two readable codes or two conflicting statements on one bottle are not a cosmetic nuisance.

Fit the correction to the whole bottle

The overlabel must sit flat on the existing label, stay clear of the shoulder, cap and tamper feature, and avoid creating an edge that catches in a shipper. Measure on a labeled production bottle, not an empty container from a catalogue. Check where the old seam lands: a second seam placed on top of it may make a thick ridge or create a lifting edge.

A full wrap may conceal more information but demands a more careful registration trial. A smaller patch may be simpler but leaves more chances for a remnant of the old message to show. Neither answer is automatic. Use the bottle measurement guide for the base dimensions, then test the additional layer at the real position.

Give rework staff a clear accept/reject sample. Hold any bottle with wrinkled placement, exposed old text or a displaced information panel. Do not treat a carton of loose overlabels as evidence that all bottles were corrected. Our artwork-version guide offers a broader way to keep revisions from crossing markets or SKUs.

What to send for a useful quote

Send a photograph of the currently labeled bottle from front, back and seam; the proposed replacement artwork; diameter and straight-wall height; quantity by SKU and lot; target market; and a note identifying the darkest information to conceal. Ask for applied samples and an opacity check before bulk printing. You may need different constructions for a paper original and a glossy film original; test the chosen adhesive on the real surface.

My rule is that a correction label must make the finished package clearer, not merely cheaper to rework. If the old and new copy still compete, the job is not ready to ship.
