On a flat presentation sheet, a holographic strip looks like a finished security seal. On a small jar, the strip must climb one curve, cross the moving cap line, bend again and remain in contact with the bottle. A stiff or badly placed construction can tent above that path. I would judge the sample on the closed container, not from the sparkle of an unused sheet.

This is a different problem from the corrugated-carton seal discussed earlier. There, the substrate can tear. Here, the issue may be curvature, cap movement, plastic surface energy or a smooth glass finish. The desired opening evidence still needs to be defined, but first the label has to reach and hold the intended contact points.

Map the opening path before choosing the seal shape

Bring the exact filled or empty sample pack with its final cap. Close it to the normal torque and mark where the cap skirt ends and the bottle wall begins. Ask what part actually moves when someone opens the container. A seal stuck to only the cap can be decorative; a seal bridging the cap and bottle may provide visible disturbance if the material and adhesive are suited to both surfaces. A decorative holographic label, however, is not automatically tamper-evident.

Try a narrow strip and a purpose-cut shape as alternatives. The narrow strip may bend more easily but offer less printable area. The custom shape may carry a stronger logo and serial cue but bridge a compound curve badly. Do not specify a universal seal width from a photograph. Measure the cap diameter, cap-wall height, shoulder radius and the area below the thread where the lower end can actually bond.

Choose flex and adhesive for both contact surfaces

HERMA's curved-surface guidance emphasizes flexible materials and secure adhesion on small diameters. Avery Dennison's film guide distinguishes rigid and more conformable films for complex package shapes. Neither source establishes that any particular RP holographic construction will pass on this jar. Material choice has to be tested with the holographic layer, adhesive and final finish together.

The two ends of a seal may meet different materials: a PP cap and a PET or glass body, for example. Ask for the actual cap resin, bottle resin and any varnish or surface treatment. Cleanliness and application pressure matter, but increasing pressure cannot flatten a design that is geometrically too wide for the cap rim. A material with a dramatic optical effect may require a compromise in flexibility; tell the printer which outcome matters most.

Test opening evidence without overstating it

Apply candidate constructions to several real containers using the intended hand or machine process. Inspect for tenting, bubbles and edge lift at application, after the specified dwell and after normal storage or handling. Open the jar and record what remains on each surface. Is the evidence immediately visible to a buyer? Can someone open from another point without disturbing the seal? If the result is ambiguous, revise the placement or construction.

What I would send for a quote

Provide the actual capped jar, intended seal location, material of both contact surfaces, holographic artwork, desired evidence after opening, roll or sheet format and quantity per design. Say whether a QR-like symbol or serial must also fit; too many features on a tiny cap bridge may leave none legible. Ask for an applied sample rather than approving a flat foil swatch.

Use the custom holographic-label inquiry to attach photos of the closed and opened pack. The best-looking sample is the one that remains properly seated until opening and then changes in the agreed way, not simply the one that throws the most rainbow light under a desk lamp.
