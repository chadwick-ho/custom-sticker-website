A supplier says the vial is 35 mm tall, so a 30 mm high label looks safe. Then the physical sample arrives: the aluminum crimp cap, narrow neck, shoulder and rounded glass base have eaten most of the useful height. The label either climbs the shoulder or covers the viewing area. I would take the measuring tool to the vial before asking a designer to make the type smaller.

The relevant dimension is the straight, consistently labelable part of the actual container. Total bottle height is only a starting point. Research vials vary by manufacturer and cap style, and a label that fit one 5 mL sample may not fit another. The height needs to be approved with the same cap and glass geometry that production will use.

Measure from the real crimped vial

Set a finished, capped vial upright. Mark the first point below the shoulder where the wall becomes sufficiently straight, then mark the start of the base radius. The distance between those marks is the candidate label band, not necessarily the final label height. Leave a visible tolerance for placement variation and any handling grip. Send clear front and side photographs with a ruler next to the vial.

Ask the packer whether the cap is crimped before or after labeling. The answer may affect access to the straight wall and line setup. HERMA's vial-labeling overview notes that wrap-around labeling is common for small cylindrical vials and can require precise positioning, including when a fill-level scale is used. That observation supports a sample-and-measure approach; it does not create a universal clearance number.

Decide whether the contents must stay visible

Some buyers want the label to hide an unattractive fill line. Others need a clear window to inspect a powder cake, liquid level or container condition. Those are different briefs. If visual inspection matters, mark the exact viewing band on the sample vial and keep critical copy away from it. A clear label is not automatically a clear window if it carries opaque white ink or dark artwork.

Do not place a QR or barcode on a severe curve simply because the flat artwork has room. GS1's General Specifications explain how a curved surface can make barcode extremes disappear from one scanner view. A tiny vial also forces tradeoffs between code size, quiet zone, human-readable identity and brand design. If the code cannot be tested reliably on the actual applied sample, put it on the outer carton or rethink the information hierarchy. The size chart is a useful first pass, not a substitute for a scannable physical proof.

Watch the top and bottom edge after application

A label that just reaches the shoulder may seem acceptable while a worker presses it down. Later, a stiff film can bridge the bend or lift near the upper edge. The base radius can produce the same problem at the bottom. HERMA's curved-surface material guidance stresses flexibility and adhesion on small-diameter packages. The appropriate film and adhesive still need to be validated on the buyer's own glass, conditions and desired finish.

I would request two candidate heights on a sample sheet or short roll, apply them using the intended method, and look at the vial from straight ahead and at an angle. Turn it through a full rotation. Check text visibility, edge contact, wrap gap and code readability. This is a suggested buyer test, not a report of an RP factory pass or a fixed industry tolerance.

Buy the usable band, not the nominal bottle height

For quotation, provide a capped vial, straight-wall measurement, target label width and height, wrap direction, required viewing window and any carton that must match the vial. If another bottle supplier might be used later, mention that now. A millimeter saved in height can be more valuable than another line of marketing copy when it keeps the top edge off the shoulder.

Send the vial reference through the custom peptide-vial-label inquiry and ask for an applied-size proof. Once the physical fit is settled, typography and finish can do their job without fighting the glass.
