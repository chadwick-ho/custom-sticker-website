A color bar is a useful signal, not a product identity system. When several small vials are packed on the same bench, a green stripe can help an operator reach for the right tray. It cannot prove that the printed name, lot field and carton all belong together.

I would decide what needs to be caught before buying a more elaborate inspection camera. The risks are usually mundane: a roll from the previous SKU left on the machine, a label placed too far around the bottle, an unreadable variable field or a correct vial placed in the wrong carton. These do not all have the same detection method.

Separate artwork approval from applied-pack inspection

The artwork proof confirms that the approved file contains the right elements. It cannot show how the label sits on a curved glass vial. An applied-pack check compares the finished vial with the approved reference: product name, version, color, lot location, orientation, adhesion and any paired outer-box identity. Keep one signed reference for each SKU instead of relying on memory.

For a three-SKU family, I would put the fixed product name in the same position across all three and let a secondary color distinguish them. The color-coding guide explains why color should aid recognition without carrying the entire meaning. A black-and-white photograph of the sample is a useful quick challenge: if two SKUs become indistinguishable without color, the names need stronger hierarchy.

Know what the camera can and cannot prove

Machine vision can be appropriate when volumes or mix risk justify it, but the pass/fail rule needs a specific target. Cognex describes optical character verification as comparing imaged characters with those configured as expected, including lot and date fields. That is different from merely detecting that a dark patch exists. A camera trained only to see a label can miss the wrong label.

HERMA describes equipment that can register a missing, misplaced or wrong label and send rejected products to a separate path. Those are capabilities of a particular configuration, not proof that any installed line does so. Ask an integrator to demonstrate the failure cases on your vials with your approved artwork, speed and lighting. Record which fault is detected by a sensor, which by vision, and which still needs an operator.

Build deliberate challenge samples

Set aside a correct vial for each SKU and a small controlled set of wrong examples: wrong SKU label, shifted label, crooked label, missing label and a lot field that cannot be read. Run these against the proposed inspection method before accepting it. Never let deliberate challenge samples return to saleable or releasable stock. A detected fault is only useful if the reject can be traced and kept separate.

Small batches may be better served by a disciplined manual check than a poorly configured camera. Check the first finished vial after every roll and SKU change, then at a frequency your own risk assessment supports. Where lots are printed later, link this check to the variable-data release process; visual resemblance does not prove a field is correct.

Ask for an inspection-ready label, not a camera promise

For a quote, send the complete SKU matrix, reference artwork, vial size, proposed application method and what must be machine-readable. Ask whether the roll layout and label surface preserve the name and lot in a visible zone on the actual curved vial. If a vision system will be used, share its camera view and approved field positions before the print die is locked.

My buying rule is plain: every SKU needs a human-readable identity that survives a poor viewing angle, and every automated rejection claim needs a challenge sample that demonstrates it. RP can help plan the printed hierarchy and consistent roll family; your packing team owns the final applied-pack release criteria.
